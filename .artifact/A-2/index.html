Research report · 26 September 2026

Getting kb's search to 80%: context in every chunk, better fusion, then a small reranker

On this project's own docs, kb finds the right section first 63–67% of the time and in the top three 77%. Our own experiments put 80% top-3 within reach now. For 80% first-hit, the research points to a reranker on top of heading-path context and score-aware fusion, plus a bigger query set to prove it. A new embedding model is the answer for code, not for docs.

Where it misses: sections that are pure reference and never say what they are about (.lapignore is all glob syntax); chunks embedded without their document title or heading path; RRF letting a middling keyword rank out-vote a clear semantic winner.

2. The answer: not a bigger model, first

80% at hit@3 is reachable with a change to fusion alone.

Re-blending kb's own rankings reaches 0.80 hit@3 on both stores, with no re-indexing.

For 80% at hit@1: context in the chunks, score-aware fusion, then a reranker.

Heading-path prefixes are the cheapest proven gain (MRR@5 0.374 → 0.463 on a 1,600-query Markdown knowledge base). A 22.7M-parameter cross-encoder reranking the top 20 is the step aimed at exactly our "right answer at rank 2–4" misses (+11% nDCG@10 averaged over BEIR).

Splitting is not the bottleneck for docs, and the embedding model is not the first lever.

kb already splits by heading; the misses are sections whose text does not state their meaning, which no model can recover. A better model does matter for code: CodeRankEmbed, same architecture and vocabulary as kb's current model, scores 60.1 vs ≈47.7 on the CoIR code-retrieval benchmark.

30 queries cannot prove 80%.

The 95% interval at 0.65 is about ±0.17. Build 150+ doc and 100+ code queries before tuning anything, or the tuning fits the noise.

6. Fusion and reranking

RRF ranks by position only, so a middling keyword rank can out-vote a clear semantic winner, which is one of the misses we saw. A normalised, weighted combination keeps the score margins; its weight is learnable from little data. A reranker reads the query and each candidate together, and is best kept to the top 20–30: going deeper often hurts.

  • Our offline sweep agrees: semantic weight 0.6 with a small RRF k was best, and the gain was 1–3 queries, too small to trust at 30 queries.
  • Watch out: llama.cpp currently scores BERT cross-encoders wrongly (open PR #21729); validate kb's implementation against sentence-transformers, not llama.cpp.

7. Efficiency at scale

  • Building is the limit: at 0.58 s per chunk, 10k chunks take ~1.6 h and 1M ~6.7 days. Batched matrix kernels, skipping noisy chunks and a content-hash cache come first.
  • Search is not: hybrid search over 170 chunks took 0.22–0.31 s per query here, model loading included. Brute force over 256-dimension int8 vectors holds to ~200k chunks; a binary prescan plus exact rescore to ~1M. No HNSW below that.
  • Memory at 1M chunks: 768-d float32 3 GB, int8 768 MB, 256-d int8 256 MB; nomic loses little at 256 dimensions (62.28 → 61.04 on MTEB).

8. The plan

  1. A benchmark first: 150+ doc queries and 100+ code queries, tagged paraphrase / identifier / purpose, with bootstrap intervals. Every step below is measured on it.
  2. Chunk hygiene: real token counts, noisy chunks keyword-only, tiny sibling sections merged.
  3. Heading path and title in the embedded text and as boosted keyword fields. Expected to be the largest single gain.
  4. Agent-written context on kb_add, re-embedding only what changed.
  5. Score-aware fusion, with RRF kept as a mode.
  6. The MiniLM-L6 reranker over the top 20, behind a switch, with latency measured.
  7. Code: tree-sitter chunking, identifier-aware tokens, symbol fields, CodeRankEmbed.
  8. Only if still short: a ModernBERT block, then gte-modernbert-base and its reranker.

Avoid: tuning on 30 queries, a query-time LLM (HyDE and query2doc), semantic chunking, proposition indexing, reranking more than ~50 candidates, HNSW below ~1M chunks, non-commercial models.
